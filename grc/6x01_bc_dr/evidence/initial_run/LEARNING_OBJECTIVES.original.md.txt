# Learning objectives: Business continuity and disaster recovery

These answers address every objective in the project README. The
[task index](Cybersecurity_Academy/Defensive%20Path/grc/6x01_bc_dr/tasks/README.md) links the complete deliverables and records the limits
of the local exercise. Business tolerances, costs, and clinical rules still need
organizational approval; successful sample restoration does not prove hospital-wide
readiness.

## Technical objectives

### Conduct a BIA across the clinical environment

A Business Impact Analysis explains what stops working, who is affected, how harm
grows with time, and which supporting systems must recover first. Assess patient
safety, regulatory obligations, revenue exposure, and operational dependencies
separately. An application that produces little direct revenue can still deserve
the highest priority because other clinical systems depend on it.

The [MedDefense BIA](tasks/business_impact_analysis.md) assesses all ten supplied
systems at one, four, and 24 hours. It places seven in Tier 1 and three in Tier 2.
Network Core and Active Directory have high priority because their loss affects
several clinical services. LIS is Tier 1 because manual specimen tracking and
critical-result calls are vulnerable to delay and identification errors.

The local notes supply clinical functions and threshold guidance, but no financial
ledger or approved downtime tolerances. Hourly revenue estimates are therefore
labeled USD assumptions with sensitivity ranges, not presented as measured losses.
Shared-service estimates overlap and must not be summed. Proposed targets require
clinical, Finance, and CISO review; the signature block remains unsigned.

### Design backups that can satisfy RTO and RPO

Start from the BIA, then choose capture frequency, application consistency, recovery
chain, geographic copies, retention, keys, and verification. RPO requires a usable
point of the required age; RTO also requires enough capacity, staffing, working
dependencies, and time to validate the service. A recent backup cannot guarantee
a quick restore.

In the [architecture](tasks/backup_architecture.md), LIS has a proposed one-hour
RPO. A 30-minute snapshot interval plus a maximum 15-minute combined delivery and
verification delay gives a designed off-site point age of 45 minutes, within
60 minutes. The current nightly copy could be almost 24 hours old, so it cannot
satisfy that target. This arithmetic assumes successful jobs and bounded delay;
monitor actual verified-point age and escalate failures.

The design defines schedules and verification for all ten Tier 1 and Tier 2
systems. Each Tier 1 has a geographically separate copy. All copies use AES-256
at rest with protected keys, and TLS 1.2 or later with certificate verification
in transit. Proposed retention and destinations are explicit, while implementation
and production-sized timing remain unproven. A geographic copy and its keys must
not share every failure domain with the production system.

### Write command-level runbooks with checks and deviations

A useful recovery step includes an exact command or action, expected output,
verification, and what to do if the result differs. Define paths from the real
configuration rather than copying an old environment's assumptions. Separate
expected output from actual evidence so examples do not look like tests performed.

The [LIS runbook](tasks/recovery_runbooks.md) verifies the archive, stages SQL at
the configured path, imports into a new SQLite database, checks five rows and both
required critical records, inspects the production hostname, and validates through
an explicit local database path. The old `/recovery/lis_dump.sql` reference remains
visible as a known error. The revised procedure checks the exact UTF-8 marker
before repairing a manifest copy and never permits a checksum mismatch to be waived.

The AD runbook starts with `netdom query fsmo` and a recovery-branch decision.
Healthy DC02 normally supports a nonauthoritative recovery or clean replacement
of DC01. The documented system-state branch includes backup selection, recovery
mode, `wbadmin`, replication checks, diagnostics, and dependent-login acceptance.
Authoritative recovery and FSMO seizure require separate criteria; their commands
must not be applied blindly. AD commands were not executed on this Linux host.

### Execute and document a timed LIS recovery

Begin timing before the first recovery step, keep the clock running through
diagnosis and correction, and save completed steps immediately. Preserve exact
commands, stdout, stderr, timestamps, and validation results. State what was staged
before timing and which real-world activities were excluded.

The [actual local test](tasks/recovery_test_record.md) recovered the supplied
five-row SQL sample and verified these required records:

```text
MRN-10043|BMP|K+: 6.1 mEq/L|1
MRN-10045|TROPONIN|0.08 ng/mL|1
```

The timer measured 245,711,516 nanoseconds, approximately 0.246 seconds, and
conservatively displayed one second. All steps fit within the same displayed
clock second; high-resolution metadata preserves the nonzero duration. The
original manifest check failed because of a leading text-encoding marker, then
passed with a separately normalized copy. That error was saved rather than hidden.

The result is CONDITIONAL PASS for local artifact restoration. No prescribed SSH
container, full LIS service, production dataset, analyzer/interface operation,
clinical acceptance, or delivered notification was tested. The sample's April
backup date also cannot demonstrate a current one-hour RPO.

## Conceptual objectives

### Distinguish RTO, RPO, and MTD

These are different business decisions, all expressed in time:

| Measure | Question answered | Proposed LIS value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RTO | How soon must a defined usable service return after interruption? | 30 minutes. |
| RPO | How old may the restored data be relative to the interruption? | 60 minutes. |
| MTD | How long can the service remain disrupted before unacceptable business or clinical harm? | 120 minutes, pending clinical approval. |

A 30-minute restore does not mean only 30 minutes of data could be lost. A one-hour
backup point does not imply the system will restore in one hour. RTO must be below
MTD, and every dependency must fit inside the end-to-end recovery clock. The gap
between the LIS target and MTD is 90 minutes, but staff must begin safe downtime
work immediately, not wait for that margin to expire.

These planning distinctions are consistent with
[NIST contingency guidance](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-34r1.pdf).
The proposed values are MedDefense scenario judgments, not NIST-prescribed targets.

### Explain dependency-based restoration order

A restored application can remain unusable because authentication, routing, backup
catalogs, or another interface is unavailable. Dependency recovery therefore starts
with facilities and protected connectivity, then identity and backup access,
then application cores and data, and finally verified interfaces and clinical
release. Some work can run in parallel; dependent service clocks do not restart
when a prerequisite finishes.

MedDefense also has a circular risk: backup access depends on identity, while
identity recovery may require the backup platform. The proposed architecture
breaks that loop with independent recovery credentials, offline bootstrap
configuration, and separately recoverable keys/catalogs. The existing materials
do not prove this design is deployed, so bootstrap testing remains open.

Epic and LIS exchange data in both directions. Restore their cores and databases
without waiting for every interface, then connect verified endpoints and reconcile
queues. Restoring systems alphabetically or declaring a database ready before
its clinical interfaces work would miss these constraints.

### Connect timing gaps to specific deficiencies

A useful finding names the failed assumption and its correction. In this exercise,
the old staging path was absent, the configuration retained a production hostname,
and the old procedure lacked dataset acceptance checks. Execution also exposed a
manifest encoding problem that the condition cards did not list.

The [gap analysis](tasks/gap_analysis.md) connects each issue to measured command
handling and the BIA's clinical thresholds. It does not invent a causal number of
minutes lost: the exercise has no equivalent clean baseline that isolates each
condition's added delay. Five extra minutes would consume 16.7% of the LIS RTO,
but the materials do not supply patient arrival rates or injury probabilities.
Additional downtime can therefore be quantified as exposure time, not as invented
patient harm counts.

Keep the 30-minute target provisionally, improve the workflow, and test full
service at realistic scale. The tiny sample's fast result supports neither a
shorter hospital recovery promise nor the conclusion that all dependencies meet
the current target.

## Transversal objectives

### Write clinical procedures that work without IT knowledge

Clinical staff need short ordered actions: who declares downtime, where forms are,
how to identify patients and specimens, whom to call, and how to reconcile work
when service returns. Use familiar role names and telephone extensions; keep
technical commands in administrator runbooks. Bold the actions whose omission
would create the greatest risk.

The [clinical references](tasks/clinical_downtime_procedures.md) provide separate
Epic and LIS procedures, each between 350 and 500 words. They name actual binder,
drawer, and tray locations from the supplied materials. The LIS reference includes
direct critical-value calls, read-back, a five-minute charge-nurse escalation, and
a ten-minute Nursing Supervisor escalation. Both require controlled return to
service and reconciliation of paper records. Clinical approval and a witnessed
night-shift drill remain necessary before deployment.

### Translate a recovery result into a board decision

Leadership needs the service risk, what the test established, what remains unknown,
and a decision with resources and accountability. A failed result must keep its
actual miss visible. A successful limited test must not be inflated into enterprise
readiness; both kinds of result can reveal important weaknesses.

The [board brief](tasks/ciso_dr_brief.md) reports the measured local result,
explains that nightly backups conflict with the proposed data-loss target, and
asks for a costed investment proposal and allocated technical and clinical
exercise staff. It lists owners and dates without inventing a budget or approval.
It presents the test's limited success and remaining production gaps together.

### Produce an independently reviewable HIPAA contingency package

Connect the evidence chain: BIA → backup architecture → runbooks → clinical
continuity → timed test → gap analysis → master plan. A reviewer should be able
to trace a recovery objective to its design, execution evidence, limitations,
and corrective action without relying on verbal reassurance.

The [master plan](tasks/dr_plan_master.md) covers scope, exclusions, roles,
activation triggers, ordered notifications, recovery gates, document versions,
testing/revision schedule, and CISO sign-off. It maps the documents to all five
specifications in the local `hipaa_164_308_a7_reference.md`: backup, recovery,
emergency-mode operation, testing/revision, and criticality analysis.

The package supports those obligations but does not prove every requirement is
implemented. Testing and criticality analysis are addressable specifications;
applicability decisions must still be assessed and documented. Approval, trained
staff, realistic end-to-end tests, and corrected backup schedules remain necessary.
See [45 CFR 164.308(a)(7)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-C/section-164.308).
